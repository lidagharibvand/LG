# Capstone research projects

From *Modern Biostatistics for the Health Sciences, Volume I*.

The projects below are designed to integrate the concepts from across Volume I. Each one asks you to take a research question through the full lifecycle covered in the book: refining the question, choosing a design, addressing bias and ethics, calculating the sample size, drafting a manuscript outline, and preparing a short presentation. The projects vary in scope; some can be completed by an individual in a few weeks, while others are better suited to a small team across a semester. None requires you to actually collect data, the deliverable is a complete, defensible protocol and presentation, not a finished study.

Pick a project that interests you and a mentor who can review your work. Treat the deliverables as the practice run of a real grant or thesis proposal.

## Suggested Deliverables (Choose as Many as Apply)

A two-page, structured research-question statement using PICO/FINER (Chapters 1, 7).

A study-design choice with a one-page justification, including the appropriate position on the hierarchy of evidence (Chapter 3).

A draft data-collection plan, with measurement instruments and a discussion of validity and reliability (Chapters 5, 5).

A sample-size calculation, including assumptions, formula or software used, sensitivity table, and inflation for dropout or clustering (Chapter 12).

A bias-and-ethics analysis identifying the three most important threats to validity and how each will be mitigated (Chapters 6, 6).

A draft IRB protocol summary, including review pathway, consent process, and AI-data-flow disclosures (Chapter 8).

A draft IMRaD outline of the manuscript that would report the completed study (Chapter 9).

A 10-slide research talk and a one-page poster mock-up describing the planned study (Chapter 11).

## Project 1. Mobile App for Medication Adherence in Hypertension

Design a randomized trial to evaluate whether a smartphone reminder app improves medication adherence in adults newly diagnosed with hypertension.

Questions to address. Define the primary outcome (proportion adherent at 6 months by Proportion of Days Covered ≥ 80%) and the analysis. Choose between an individually randomized and a cluster-randomized design. Calculate the sample size assuming baseline adherence of 55% and a clinically meaningful improvement of 10 percentage points at 80% power.

Bias and ethics. Consider self-selection of patients willing to use the app, contamination between arms in shared households, differential dropout, and the privacy implications of medication-taking data leaving the device.

## Project 2. Patient-Experience Survey in a Primary-Care Clinic

Design and pilot a patient-experience survey for adult patients of a multi-site primary-care clinic. The clinic’s leadership wants results stratified by site and patient demographics.

Questions to address. Draft 10–12 items covering access, communication, and care coordination. Specify the response scale and decide which items are nominal, ordinal, or interval. Plan a pilot with 30 respondents, then validation with a larger sample. Calculate the sample size required to estimate site-specific satisfaction proportions to within ±5 percentage points at 95% confidence.

Bias and ethics. Consider non-response bias, social-desirability bias, and language access. Decide whether the survey can be administered anonymously and what the IRB pathway would be.

## Project 3. Qualitative Study of Burnout in Frontline Clinicians

Design a qualitative study of burnout in nurses or residents at an academic medical center. Use Chapter 2 to choose the qualitative tradition that best fits the question (e.g., phenomenology to describe lived experience, or grounded theory to explain how burnout develops).

Questions to address. Specify your sampling strategy (purposive, snowball, or maximum variation), justify the planned number of interviews, and outline a coding plan with examples of likely open codes and at least one axial code. Plan for member checking and triangulation.

Bias and ethics. Consider the power differential between investigator and participant when both work in the same institution. How will you protect identifying details in published quotes? How will sensitive disclosures (e.g., substance use, suicidal thoughts) be handled?

## Project 4. Retrospective Cohort Study Using Electronic Health Records

Propose a retrospective cohort study using de-identified electronic health record (EHR) data to compare a clinical outcome between two treatment groups (for example, two first-line antihypertensive drugs or two surgical approaches for the same condition).

Questions to address. Specify the cohort definition, the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the exposure and the outcome, and the planned adjustment strategy (propensity-score matching or multivariable regression). Identify the most important confounders. Calculate the sample size needed to detect a clinically meaningful difference.

Bias and ethics. Discuss confounding by indication, immortal-time bias, and missing data. Specify the data-use agreement and the IRB pathway for de-identified secondary data.

## Project 5. Cluster-Randomized Trial of a Clinic-Level Quality Improvement Program

Design a cluster-randomized trial in which primary-care clinics are randomized to receive a quality-improvement program (e.g., a diabetes-care bundle, a screening protocol, a structured discharge process) or to continue current practice.

Questions to address. Define the unit of randomization and the unit of analysis. Specify the primary patient-level outcome. Assume an intra-cluster correlation in the range 0.02–0.10, choose a value with citation, and calculate the inflated sample size. Discuss whether stratified or matched randomization of clinics is preferable.

Bias and ethics. Consider differential implementation across clinics, contamination, secular trends, and consent at the cluster vs. individual level.

## Project 6. Diagnostic-Accuracy Study of a New Screening Test

Design a diagnostic-accuracy study to compare a new, lower-cost screening test against an established gold standard for a condition of your choice (e.g., a point-of-care assay vs. laboratory testing, an AI-driven imaging tool vs. expert reader).

Questions to address. Define sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, and AUC for the primary outcome. Specify the population and prevalence. Calculate the sample size required to estimate sensitivity to within ±5 percentage points at the expected prevalence.

Bias and ethics. Discuss verification bias, spectrum bias, and the special considerations for AI-based tests (subgroup performance, distributional shift, fairness).

## Project 7. Non-Inferiority Trial of a Shorter Treatment Course

Design a non-inferiority trial comparing a shortened course of an established treatment (e.g., a 7-day vs. 14-day antibiotic regimen, a shorter post-surgical hospitalization) to the standard course. Non-inferiority designs require careful margin-setting and larger samples than superiority trials.

Questions to address. Choose a clinically defensible non-inferiority margin and justify it with prior literature. Decide whether intention-to-treat or per-protocol analysis is primary (non-inferiority trials usually require both). Calculate the sample size at 90% power.

Bias and ethics. Discuss the patient-level and societal motivation for studying a less-intensive option, and the risk of declaring non-inferiority on the basis of an underpowered or noisy trial.

## Project 8. Validation Study of a Self-Report Instrument

Design a validation study for a new or adapted self-report instrument, for example a culturally adapted depression screener for a specific community, or a translated version of an existing pain scale.

Questions to address. Specify which forms of validity (content, criterion, construct) and which forms of reliability (internal consistency, test-retest, inter-rater) you will evaluate. Plan a factor analysis, a Cronbach’s-alpha analysis, and a comparison against a gold-standard clinical interview. Calculate the sample size for each component.

Bias and ethics. Consider language equivalence, cultural appropriateness of items, and the IRB implications of screening for sensitive conditions during research.

## Project 9. AI-Assisted Decision-Support Tool in a Clinical Setting

Design a study to evaluate an AI decision-support tool integrated into routine clinical care, for example an early-warning score for sepsis, an imaging-based prediction tool, or an LLM-generated clinical summary.

Questions to address. Decide whether the study is a development-and-internal-validation study, an external-validation study, a clinical-utility study, or a pragmatic randomized trial of the tool. Specify the primary outcome (model performance, clinician behavior, or patient outcome) and the appropriate reporting framework (TRIPOD+AI, DECIDE-AI, CONSORT-AI).

Bias and ethics. Discuss subgroup performance and fairness, distributional shift between training and deployment data, clinician over-reliance and alert fatigue, and the IRB and consent implications of AI involvement in clinical care.

## Project 10. Public-Health Surveillance Using Routine Data

Design a public-health surveillance plan that uses routinely collected data (hospital admissions, emergency-department visits, school absences, or wastewater sampling) to detect emerging clusters of an infectious or environmental health threat in a county or state.

Questions to address. Define the case definition, the data sources, the geographic and temporal granularity, and the statistical method for detecting unusual increases (e.g., CUSUM, EWMA, or scan statistics). Discuss thresholds for action and how false alarms will be handled.

Bias and ethics. Consider underreporting in marginalized populations, geographic disparities in data quality, and privacy implications of small-area statistics.

## Final Note

These projects are intentionally open-ended. The most valuable part of working through them is not arriving at a single “right” answer but practicing the judgments that distinguish a thoughtful researcher from a careless one: which assumptions are defensible, which biases dominate, what the sample size actually needs to be, and what the study can and cannot tell us. Take the projects seriously, write up your protocols as if they were real, and present them as if you were defending them at a committee meeting. The habits you build now will follow you into every grant application, IRB submission, and manuscript you write.
