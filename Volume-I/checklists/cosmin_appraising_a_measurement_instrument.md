# D.9 COSMIN: Appraising a Measurement Instrument

From Appendix D of *Modern Biostatistics for the Health Sciences, Volume I*.

The eight sections before this one are reporting guidelines: they tell you what to write down about a study you have already done. COSMIN is a different kind of instrument, and it is the one Chapter 7 keeps pointing at. It does not ask whether a study was reported well. It asks whether a measurement instrument is any good, and whether the studies claiming it is were done well enough to believe.

COSMIN stands for COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments. It came out of a Delphi process among methodologists who could not agree on what “validity” meant from one paper to the next, and its first and most useful contribution is a vocabulary. The full checklist, its manuals and its user guides are free from the COSMIN website; what follows is a summary of what it asks and why, written for this volume, and it is not a substitute for the instrument itself.

#### The taxonomy: three domains, nine properties

COSMIN sorts everything an instrument can be judged on into three domains. Reliability asks whether the score is stable. Validity asks whether it measures what it claims. Responsiveness asks whether it detects change. Within those sit nine measurement properties, and the table below maps each one onto the sections of this volume that teach it.

Two further things COSMIN insists on naming, while refusing to call them measurement properties, are interpretability (what a score of 42 actually means, whether there are floor or ceiling effects, what counts as a meaningful change) and feasibility, which is how long the thing takes to complete, what it costs, what reading level it assumes, and whether you are allowed to use it. An instrument can be impeccable on all nine properties and still be unusable in a fifteen-minute clinic visit.

#### What COSMIN adds beyond the vocabulary

A risk-of-bias appraisal, per property.Not one judgment for the paper, but a separate judgment for each measurement property the paper reports. A study can establish reliability impeccably and test structural validity on 40 people.

Criteria for what counts as good.Separately from whether the study was well conducted, COSMIN asks whether the result itself is adequate, the thresholds in the next section.

A way to grade the whole body of evidence.Results across studies are pooled per property and graded with a modified GRADE approach, so a review can conclude “high-quality evidence for sufficient reliability, very low-quality evidence for content validity” rather than a single verdict.

That three-part structure (was the study done well, was the result good enough, and how confident can we be across studies) is the part worth carrying away even if you never open the checklist. Most instrument papers conflate all three.

#### The quality criteria, in brief

These are the thresholds most commonly applied when judging whether a reported result is adequate. They are conventions, not laws, and every one of them is defensible to depart from with a stated reason. Citing them without a reason is not.

Internal consistency.α between 0.70 and 0.95, and only after structural validity has shown the scale is unidimensional. Below 0.70 the items are not measuring one thing; above 0.95 they are probably measuring it several times over.

Reliability.ICC or weighted κ of at least 0.70, with the interval between administrations stated and defended.

Measurement error.The smallest detectable change is below the smallest change that matters clinically. A score whose noise exceeds the effect it is meant to detect cannot be used to follow a patient.

Content validity.Relevance, comprehensiveness and comprehensibility each assessed with patients as well as experts. COSMIN treats this as the most important property, and it is the one most often skipped.

Structural validity.A confirmatory model with acceptable fit, or a well-conducted exploratory analysis where no model is yet proposed.

Hypotheses testing.At least three quarters of the hypotheses stated before the analysis are confirmed. Hypotheses written after the correlations were seen establish nothing.

Cross-cultural validity.No important difference in factor structure, loadings or thresholds between the groups being compared.

Criterion validity.Correlation with the gold standard of at least 0.70, where a gold standard genuinely exists. For most patient-reported constructs it does not.

Responsiveness.At least three quarters of the change hypotheses confirmed, judged the same way as hypotheses testing.

Note

Notice how many of these are about the design of the study rather than the size of a coefficient. A Cronbach’s α of 0.91 on a scale whose dimensionality was never tested is not evidence of anything, which is the point Section 7.6 makes about item analysis coming before factor analysis.

#### A checklist for a validation study write-up

An original checklist drafted for this volume from the COSMIN framework and the ten-step pipeline of Chapter 7. Use it to audit your own manuscript before submission, or a published instrument before you adopt it.

The construct is defined before any item is written, and the definition names the population and the intended use.

Content validity is established with patients from that population, not only with clinicians and experts.

The measurement model is stated: reflective or formative, and how many dimensions are expected.

Structural validity is tested on a sample large enough for the model, and on a different sample from the one used to generate it.

Internal consistency is reported per subscale, never once for a multidimensional instrument as a whole.

Test-retest reliability names the interval and justifies it: long enough that the answer is not remembered, short enough that the construct has not changed.

Measurement error is reported in the score’s own units and compared with the smallest change that matters.

Every construct-validity hypothesis is stated in advance, with a direction and an expected magnitude.

Where scores will be compared across groups, measurement invariance is tested and the level reached is named.

Responsiveness is assessed against an external anchor, not by the instrument’s own change scores alone.

Missing-data handling and the scoring algorithm are documented well enough for another team to reproduce a score from raw responses.

The interpretation section gives the scale’s range, its floor and ceiling effects, and reference or normative values where available.

Feasibility is reported: completion time, reading level, licensing, and what it costs to administer.

Each property is reported with its sample size, so a reader can judge whether the estimate is worth anything.
